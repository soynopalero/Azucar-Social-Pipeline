The whole job, in order

  • Turn everything off at the red button under the bar. It stays off until the tank is full again.
  • Hook the changer to the bar sink. Green valve down = draining.
  • Vacuum the gravel with the long tube. Scrub the acrylic. Take out about a palm's length of water.
  • Turn the water off. Match the temperature by hand — cold first, then hot. 79°F.
  • Green valve up and screwed tight = filling. Add half a teaspoon of Safe.
  • Fill until the intakes and the power head are well under. Then turn the system back on.
  • Check the two output heads are moving water. Wipe down. Done.
Roughly 45 minutes

Longer the first few times. There is no rush in any of it — he is explicit that you can vacuum as long as you like, and that taking breaks to refill partway is normal on a heavy clean.

01 Never do these

Each of these is either his own warning or a direct consequence of one.

  • Never leave the system running while the water is down. The filters take in air, and the heater ends up in open air. His words: "it can cause issues with heating elements that's supposed to be in water. Not in water can cause corrosion. It can melt plastic."
  • Never touch the red button or the surge protector with wet hands. You are reaching into sockets behind a bar. He suggests keeping a towel with you and drying your hands before you touch anything electrical.
  • Never use the short vacuum tube for a real clean. Pebbles get pulled all the way through. They jam the green valve — which then needs a screwdriver to open and clear — or end up in the sink's P-trap. With the long tube the rocks travel halfway up and drop back.
  • Never scrub low without pushing the sand away first. The front is acrylic. One grain of sand caught under the pad leaves a permanent scratch. Use the vacuum to sweep the sand back before you clean down there.
  • Never power-wash or scrub the filter pads hard. Squeeze them out in water repeatedly instead — that is what gets the trapped material out of the foam.
  • Never drain so far the fish have nowhere to go. Stop, refill part way, add a little Safe, then carry on vacuuming.

02 Know your parts

He points at these rather than naming them. This is what he is pointing at.

Big canister under the bar

Fluval FX4 — the main filter

Draws water through the large intake tube in the tank and returns it through the two output heads. If anything else fails, this is the one that has to keep running.

Green plunger on the hose

The fill / drain valve

The single most important control. It decides whether water leaves the tank or enters it.

PositionWhat happens
Plunger downDrains. Sink water pulls tank water out and down the drain.
Up, screwed tightFills. Blocks the lower exit so sink water runs into the tank.

"So to clean you twist it counterclockwise once and then you just pull down — it requires like no force."

Valve wrapped in caution tape

The sink control valve

Turns the flow to the changer on and off. Handle down / in line means water passes. Handle sideways / across means off.

He put the tape on it himself so it could be found in a hurry.

Squat cone-shaped unit in the tank

Power head

Circulates water — it is not a filter and connects to nothing. Point it whichever way you like. The whole thing, and especially the bottom half of the cone, must stay submerged.

Long dark tube, back corner

Heater

Visibly corroded but working. This is the part that gets destroyed by running dry, so it drives the whole "system off while draining" rule.

Thin probe near the heater

Temperature probe

Reports tank temperature to the controller so the heater knows what to do. The thermostat.

Second, smaller intake tube

The internal filter

A smaller filter inside the tank with its own intake, separate from the FX4.

White jar, behind the bar

Seachem Safe

Powdered dechlorinator. Removes chlorine and helps with the ammonia that a clean stirs up. The powder is far more concentrated than the liquid, which is why he buys it for a tank this size.

04 How often

His answer: "about another two weeks to a month. Play by ear. This fish tank isn't too horribly overcrowded."

Every two weeks — light
  • Gravel vacuum. Take out roughly a quarter of what a monthly service would.
  • Don't fuss about the exact level. Getting through the gravel is the point.
Monthly — full
  • Change about 20% — three to three and a half inches down from the surface, "half a palm's length, from where your middle finger and your wrist connect."
  • Vacuum thoroughly, scrub the panels, scrub the intake strainers.
Roughly yearly — deep
  • Pull the intake strainers off and properly clean them.
  • Pick a day with few customers, or come in early.

"The amount of water you take out is less relevant, to be totally honest with you, than getting in there and doing the gravel siphon, because the dirt and the debris… is going to be trapped in this layer."

05 Setting up

  1. 1

    Turn the whole system off at the red button under the bar.

    Before any water moves. Filters, heater and power head all stay off until the tank is full again.

  2. 2

    Screw the faucet adapter onto the bar sink. If the tap has external thread, the extra adapter piece goes on first.

  3. 3

    Push the hose onto the green valve. It is a friction fit — push it home firmly.

  4. 4

    Set the caution-taped valve to across (off) while you connect things, and get a towel.

06 Draining and vacuuming

  1. 1

    Set the green valve to drain: twist counterclockwise once, pull the plunger down.

    No force needed. If it fights you, it isn't twisted round yet.

  2. 2

    Turn the caution-taped valve down, then turn the sink on.

  3. 3

    Fit the long vacuum tube and work it down into the gravel, section by section.

    You can go over the same patch as long as you like. The only limit is how much water you're removing.

  4. 4

    Before scrubbing anywhere low, use the vacuum to push the sand back from the front panel.

    A grain of sand under the pad scratches the acrylic permanently.

  5. 5

    Scrub the inside panels with the pad. One swipe, two swipe, repeat along to the end.

  6. 6

    Scrub the intake strainers, then hover the vacuum tube over them to pick up whatever you knocked loose.

  7. 7

    Stop draining about three to three and a half inches below the surface — or a quarter of that on a two-week service.

    If the level gets low enough to crowd the fish, stop early, refill part way, add a little Safe, and carry on.

07 Refilling and treating

  1. 1

    Turn the water off at the sink. Leave the system off.

  2. 2

    Put your hand in the tank and get a feel for the temperature.

    You are matching what the fish are already sitting in, not hitting a number cold.

  3. 3

    At the tap, turn the cold on first, all the way, then bring the hot in slowly until it feels about the same. Target 79°F.

    "It's not like a science — if you're degrees off your fish aren't gonna die. I want it to be close."

  4. 4

    Turn the green piece to fill — up and screwed tight, clockwise.

  5. 5

    Leave the hose down in the tank while it fills.

    Stops it splashing and making a mess across the bar.

  6. 6

    Add half a teaspoon of Safe. Anywhere in the tank is fine.

    The label says a quarter teaspoon per 300 gallons; he doses a full half for this tank because the margin is harmless. It circulates through the whole system, so it does not matter where it goes in — only that it goes in while you are filling.

  7. 7

    Fill until the black intake and the power head's cone are well under water.

    This is the gate for turning the system back on. Not level with the surface — properly submerged.

08 Finishing up

  1. 1

    At the level you want, turn the green piece to close. Filling stops.

  2. 2

    Walk to the sink and open the valve to drain.

    Pressure sometimes pops the green piece open by itself — that is normal, not a fault. Either way the hose needs draining.

  3. 3

    Pull the hose out, disengage the stop, and let the water run out of it.

    A hose left wet deteriorates and starts to smell of mildew. It doesn't have to be bone dry — just not holding water.

  4. 4

    Turn the sinks off, disconnect the adapter, put the kit away.

  5. 5

    Dry your hands, then turn the system back on at the red button.

  6. 6

    Watch the two output heads until you can see them pushing water.

    That is the confirmation that the FX4 has primed and you are actually finished.

  7. 7

    Wipe down the counter and the outside of the tank.

09 Servicing the FX4

Not part of every clean. Separate job, done with the system off.

  1. 1

    There are two baskets. Each holds a round black pad and two half-circle sponge pads — three per basket, six pads in total.

  2. 2

    Fill each pad with water, squeeze it out, and repeat.

    "I let it fill up with water, I squeeze it out, I let it fill up and I squeeze it out, and I repeat that a few times — usually by like your tenth time it's gonna run pretty clear."

    Close to clear is the target. A slight tinge is fine.

  3. 3

    Intake strainers come off by friction — twist and pull with enough force. Run them under hot water for a proper clean.

    Only worth doing about once a year. A baking-soda soak and an old toothbrush is his suggestion for that annual pass.

Unresolved — rinse in tank water until he confirms

He says he runs the pads "under water" without ever specifying tap or tank water. For plain mechanical foam that is usually fine, but if any of those six pads is carrying the bacterial colony, rinsing it under the tap kills it and the tank spikes days later. Rinsing in a bucket of water you just drained is safe either way, so do that until he answers.

10 If something looks wrong

  1. 1

    No water from the output heads after you switch back on. Check the water is well above the intakes, then that the system really is powered.

    Most likely it took in air because the level was too low when it started.

  2. 2

    The green valve won't drain or fill. Suspect a pebble jammed in it.

    He warns about exactly this — it opens with a screwdriver so it can be cleared. Use the long tube next time.

  3. 3

    Water is cloudy after a clean. Expected. Leave it.

    You have just stirred the gravel. The Safe covers the ammonia that comes with it.

  4. 4

    The sink drains slowly afterwards. Gravel may have reached the P-trap.

  5. 5

    Water on the floor. Dry your hands, kill the power at the surge protector, then find it.

11 Ask him

Genuine gaps in the handover, worth a phone call while he is still willing to take one.

  • The filter pads — tank water or tap? The one answer that could cost the tank. Also: which of the six are biological and which are purely mechanical?
  • Feeding. Never covered anywhere in the video. What, how much, how often — and whether the pleco needs anything the others don't.
  • Water testing. Never mentioned. Does he test at all, and with what?
  • Where he buys Safe and replacement FX4 pads, and how often pads get replaced rather than rinsed.
  • The heater. He notes it is visibly corroded but working. How long has it been like that, and when would he replace it?
  • What the fish are, so illness can be looked up. The tank appears to hold blood parrot cichlids, convict cichlids and at least one pleco.
