What to do tomorrow

  • Do a partial water change, about a quarter of the tank.
  • Do vacuum the visible debris off the substrate while you drain.
  • Do clean the inside glass and refill to the old water line.
  • Do photograph the filter and every label before you touch it.
  • Do service the filter only if you can identify the type in section 07 and follow it start to finish.
  • Don't change any setting — heater dial, timer, valve position, pump speed.
  • Don't replace all the filter media at once, or add anything to the water but dechlorinator.
If you only do one thing

Do the water change. It delivers most of the benefit, it is hard to get wrong, and a tank that gets a quarter of its water changed and nothing else is in good shape. The filter can wait two weeks for the person who knows it.

Except if the water isn't moving

No flow from the outlet, no ripple on the surface — the filter has stopped and that is urgent. Go to If something looks wrong.

01 Never do these

Each one kills fish, and each is true of every freshwater tank — so these hold even without his instructions.

  • Never rinse filter media under the tap. Chlorine kills the bacteria living in that media, and those bacteria are what keep the fish alive. Media gets rinsed in a bucket of water taken out of the tank, never anything else.
  • Never use soap, detergent, glass cleaner, or anything that has touched them. Residue is lethal and doesn't rinse off. Buckets, scrapers and towels used on this tank get used on nothing else, ever.
  • Never put untreated tap water into the tank. Same chlorine, straight into the fish. Every drop gets dechlorinator first, including top-offs for evaporation.
  • Never drain below the heater while it's plugged in. A heater running in air overheats and then cracks when cool water returns. Unplug it before any water leaves the tank.
  • Never replace all the filter media at the same time. The colony lives on that media. Replacing everything at once restarts the tank from zero and causes an ammonia spike days later. One component per service, at most.
  • Never force anything that doesn't come apart easily. It's held by a clip or a lock you can't see. Photograph it and stop.
  • Never use hand sanitizer, soap, or lotion before putting hands in the tank. Rinse bare hands in plain water.

02 Why any of this matters

Two minutes of background. It's what lets you make a sensible call when this page doesn't cover what you're looking at.

Fish produce ammonia, which is poisonous to them. In an established tank, a colony of bacteria living on the filter media converts that ammonia into nitrite, and a second colony converts the nitrite into nitrate, which is comparatively harmless. That chain is the entire reason the tank works, and it takes weeks to build.

Nearly every rule here protects that colony. It lives on surfaces — filter sponges, ceramic media, gravel, decorations — not in the water. That's why you can change water freely, but must never rinse media under the tap, replace all of it at once, or scrub the whole tank down.

Nitrate is the one thing the bacteria don't remove, so it accumulates. Removing nitrate is what a water change is for. That's the job, and it's why the water change is the part that actually can't be skipped.

03 How often

Standard maintenance rhythm. Confirm against his schedule when he's reachable — he may have run this tank differently.

Every day
  • Feed a small pinch — see section 09.
  • Look at the fish for a few seconds. Are they behaving normally?
  • Check the water is moving.
Every week
  • Change 20–25% of the water.
  • Vacuum a section of the substrate — a different section each week.
  • Scrape the inside glass, wipe the outside with plain water.
  • Check the temperature against what it normally reads.
Every month
  • Rinse the mechanical filter media in old tank water.
  • Clean the impeller — this is usually why flow drops off.
  • Test the water if a kit is on site.
As needed
  • Top off evaporation with dechlorinated water. Evaporation removes water but leaves minerals behind, so top-offs are not a substitute for water changes.
  • Replace filter floss or carbon when matted and grey — one component at a time.

05 The water change

The main job, and the safe one. Aim for roughly a quarter of the tank.

  1. 1

    Mark the current water line and read the thermometer. Write the temperature down.

    You're refilling to this exact line at this same temperature. Nothing about the setup changes today.

  2. 2

    Unplug the heater. Unplug the filter and any pump.

    Both are damaged by running dry. Do this before any water leaves the tank.

    Leave them unplugged

    Everything stays off until the tank is full again. Plugging the heater in early is the most common way one gets cracked.

  3. 3

    Start the siphon into the bucket, and push the wide tube into the gravel a few inches at a time. Let the debris lift out, then move along.

    Waste sitting in the substrate is most of what fouls the water. Cover about a third of the floor — the rest waits for next week.

  4. 4

    Stop draining at about a quarter of the tank.

    Bigger changes swing the chemistry. Leave the fish in — netting them out is far more stressful than the water change itself.

  5. 5

    Fill a clean bucket with tap water, add dechlorinator at the dose on the label, and bring it to the temperature you wrote down.

    Match within a degree or two. Test it against the inside of your wrist — it should feel neither warm nor cold.

  6. 6

    Pour slowly, onto a rock, a decoration, or your flat hand held over the substrate.

    Poured straight down it craters the gravel and clouds the tank for a day.

  7. 7

    Refill to your mark. Plug the filter back in, then the heater.

    Filter first, so you can confirm flow returns before walking away.

  8. 8

    Watch for two minutes. Water should be moving and the fish should settle.

    If flow doesn't return, the filter has probably lost its prime — see section 10.

06 Glass and decorations

  1. 1

    Scrape the inside glass before draining, so the algae you loosen leaves with the old water.

  2. 2

    Leave the decorations and any live plants where they are.

    They carry bacteria too, and rearranging them stresses territorial fish. Today is maintenance, not redecorating.

  3. 3

    Wipe the outside glass with plain water on a paper towel.

    Not glass cleaner

    Ammonia-based sprays near an open tank are dangerous. Plain water, outside only.

Check before scraping

A blade or rough pad permanently scratches acrylic. Tap the side gently: glass rings, acrylic sounds dull, and acrylic edges look thick and slightly rounded. If you aren't certain, use a soft pad and note what you saw.

07 The filter

Find yours below. If none of them match what you're looking at, do the water change and leave the filter for him.

Before you open anything

Unplug it. Photograph it from four sides with every hose, valve and cable in frame, and photograph any label or model number. If it comes apart into pieces, photograph the order they came out in. You are the only person who will see the inside of this thing for two weeks.

Hangs on the back rim, water spills back in

Hang-on-back

  1. Unplug it.
  2. Lift the cartridge or sponge straight out and put it in a bucket of water you just drained from the tank.
  3. Squeeze and swish it in that bucket until the water clouds and the media runs clearer. It does not need to look new — grubby media is working media.
  4. If it's a paper-and-carbon cartridge that's falling apart, replace it — but only if there's a sponge or bio-wheel staying behind. If the cartridge is the only media, rinse it and keep it.
  5. Pull the impeller out of its well (a small magnet with fins), rinse the hair and grit off it, and put it back the same way round.
  6. Refill the box with tank water before plugging in. Most need that to restart.
Sealed cylinder in the cabinet, two hoses running up

Canister

  1. Unplug it. Close both valves, or use the quick-release to disconnect the hoses.
  2. Carry the whole canister to a bucket or a sink — it is heavier and fuller than it looks.
  3. Release the clamps and lift the lid. Lift the media baskets out, keeping their order.
  4. Rinse the coarse sponges and any floss in old tank water. Replace the floss if grey and matted.
  5. Leave the ceramic rings and bio-balls alone. Only if visibly clogged, swish them gently in tank water — never scrub them, never replace them.
  6. Clean the impeller and its shaft. This is almost always why flow has dropped.
  7. Find the large O-ring on the lid. Wipe it clean, check it isn't twisted or pinched, and seat it fully in its groove. A smear of silicone grease if there's some to hand.
  8. Reassemble, reconnect, open the valves, prime it (a primer button, or fill the canister with water first), and plug in.
  9. Stay and watch it for five minutes, hand under the joints.
The O-ring is the flood

A canister lid closed on a twisted O-ring will empty the tank onto the floor overnight. If it doesn't seat cleanly, don't close it — take the photos and leave the filter out of service rather than risk it.

Sponge on a tube inside the tank, bubbles rising

Sponge or internal

  1. Unplug the pump, or leave the air pump running — it can't run dry.
  2. Lift the sponge out and into a bucket of old tank water.
  3. Squeeze it out several times until the water runs clearer. That's the whole job.
  4. Put it straight back. Don't let it dry out.
Open tank below the display, water overflowing down into it

Sump

  1. Leave it. Rinse or replace the filter sock or floss pad if there's an obvious dirty one, in old tank water.
  2. Everything else waits for him — sumps have shut-off sequences that flood a room when done in the wrong order.

08 Testing the water

Only if a kit is on site. Skip it otherwise — don't buy anything today.

  • Ammonia — should be zero. Anything above zero needs acting on.
  • Nitrite — should be zero. Same.
  • Nitrate — will show something, and that's normal. Water changes bring it down.
  • pH — record it, don't adjust it. Chasing pH with additives does more harm than a steady slightly-off number.
If ammonia or nitrite reads above zero

Change another quarter of the water in a day or two, and feed lightly until it reads zero. It usually means the bacteria colony was disturbed. Note the number and the date.

09 Feeding

Underfeeding is nearly harmless. Overfeeding fouls the water and is the most common way a healthy tank goes bad.

  • A small pinch once a day.
  • It should be gone in two minutes. Food still on the gravel afterwards means too much went in.
  • Skip feeding on water-change day if they've already eaten.
  • A healthy tank is fine unfed over a long weekend. Don't leave a big feed to compensate.
Not yet known

What he feeds them, how much, how often, and whether any of these fish need something specific — bottom feeders often need food that sinks. Use the container already on site at the amount above until he confirms.

10 If something looks wrong

  1. 1

    No water movement. Check it's plugged in and switched on, then that the valves match your photo, then whether the impeller is jammed.

    Filters often lose their prime after a water change and need restarting. If you can't restore flow, this is the one thing worth calling for help about today — a tank with no filtration is on a clock, though it has days rather than hours.

  2. 2

    Fish at the surface gulping. Confirm flow, don't feed, and change another quarter of the water.

    Usually low oxygen or a water quality problem. Both improve with fresh water and surface movement.

  3. 3

    Cloudy water after the change. Leave it alone.

    Usually disturbed substrate, and it clears within a day. A milky haze a few days later is a bacterial bloom, which also passes. Adding chemicals to fix either makes it worse.

  4. 4

    A fish is dead. Take it out promptly, note the date and anything unusual about it.

    A body left in decays and spikes ammonia.

  5. 5

    Water on the floor. Unplug everything electrical first, then find the leak.

  6. 6

    White spots, torn fins, or fish scraping on decorations. Photograph it and note it. Don't medicate.

    Treating the wrong disease is worse than waiting. Clean water is the right first response to almost every fish illness.
